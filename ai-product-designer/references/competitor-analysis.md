# Competitor Analysis Framework

## Purpose

Provide a repeatable methodology for analysing competing products, identifying experience gaps, and discovering opportunities for differentiation.

## 1. Define the competitive landscape

Classify competitors into:

- Direct competitors: Solve a similar problem for a similar audience.
- Indirect competitors: Solve the same problem through a different approach.
- Emerging alternatives: New products or technologies that could change user expectations.

Define the target geography, customer segment, product category, and evaluation scope.

## 2. Establish comparison criteria

Select relevant dimensions before researching.

| Dimension | What to evaluate |
|---|---|
| Onboarding | Registration and initial setup |
| Navigation | Information architecture |
| Core journey | Main task completion |
| Usability | Ease of understanding and use |
| Trust | Transparency, security and reassurance |
| Personalisation | Adaptation to user needs |
| Accessibility | Inclusive interaction |
| Support | Help and issue resolution |
| AI capabilities | Automation and intelligent assistance |

Not every dimension applies to every product.

## 3. Evidence collection

For each competitor, capture:

- Product name
- Official website or app
- Research date
- User segment
- Screenshots or observations
- Relevant features
- Journey stages
- Supporting source links
- Unverified information

Use official product sources and direct product observations wherever possible.

Distinguish observed features from marketing claims.

Never invent competitor capabilities.

## 4. Comparison matrix

Use this structure:

| Evaluation area | Product A | Product B | Product C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Registration | Observed finding | Observed finding | Unknown |
| Verification | Observed finding | Observed finding | Unknown |
| Core experience | Observed finding | Observed finding | Unknown |
| Support | Observed finding | Observed finding | Unknown |
| Differentiation | Evidence-based finding | Evidence-based finding | Evidence-based finding |

Mark unavailable evidence as unknown instead of guessing.

## 5. Identify experience gaps

Look for:

- Unnecessary steps
- Repeated data entry
- Unclear instructions
- Poor feedback
- Missing recovery paths
- Inconsistent interactions
- Accessibility barriers
- Weak trust signals

Evaluate gaps based on their impact on users, not visual preference alone.

## 6. Discover product opportunities

Translate observations into opportunities.

**Observed gap:** What is missing or difficult?

**User impact:** Why does it matter?

**Business relevance:** What outcome could improve?

**Opportunity:** What could the product do differently?

**Validation:** What evidence is still needed?

## 7. Prioritisation

Evaluate opportunities using:

- User value
- Business impact
- Differentiation potential
- Implementation effort
- Technical feasibility
- Risk

Present recommendations with reasoning and trade-offs.

## 8. Final deliverables

Depending on the task, produce:

1. Competitor shortlist
2. Competitive landscape
3. Comparison matrix
4. Experience gap analysis
5. Opportunity areas
6. Prioritised recommendations
7. Source list with research dates

## Quality standards

- Never present assumptions as facts.
- Never fabricate product features or statistics.
- Include source links for externally researched findings.
- Clearly state the research date.
- Identify limitations in available evidence.
- Do not confuse visual similarity with equivalent usability.

## Guiding principle

Competitor analysis should help identify opportunities to create better user experiences, not encourage copying competitors.
